The Hand But Follows

There has always been a frustration native to the artist: taste and intuition arrive first, and they arrive uninvited. You hear the phrase before you can play it. You see the painting before you mix the colors. You feel the form long before you can sculpt it.

Our senses grasp models of excellence instantly, yet the hand but takes a lifetime to follow.

For eleven years I played the violin, and for a significant portion I was self-taught outside of the base elements. The instrument does not meet you halfway.

A fretless instrument remains unforgiving in a way that doesn't diminish with experience. Whereas the beginner fumbles toward mustering a note or jump; the expert agonizes over the same note to render it exactly to their liking or interpretation. For the first years, comfort is a stranger as to even stand or hold the instrument exacts some baseline contortion. Your neck cranes to anchor the chin; the wrist hitches to endure your self-interrogation, and bit-by-bit you feel yourself less a musician than a marionette suspended by slack strings: an awkward child of an inattentive puppeteer.

Everything registers itself as a series of foreign sensations, yet what's wondrous is that the body and the mind adapt. As you writhe and will the sound in your head into being, you place a finger in the general vicinity on the fret and find yourself a hundred cents off. Sometimes higher, sometimes lower.

Then there is more to holding a bow than one might think: where the thumb anchors, where your pinky perches, how your index curls and lifts, how your middle and ring fingers drape the frog. All this before you've drawn a single note. Then comes where on the string you draw, the angle of contact, the pressure, the speed. Again, all this before you can concern yourself with anything beyond the fundamentals.

It was not until one of my first private lessons in my senior year of high school, eight years in, that my teacher corrected how I had been holding the bow. My index like this. My pinky like so. The adjustment was small. The difference in sound was not. Years of practicing a subtly wrong technique and I had no way of knowing, because the error was beneath what I could yet hear.

Even now, as I return to the instrument between bouts of research, I find the body is no longer prepared. That boundary between familiarity and discomfort has weathered thin. The lower back betrays the posture; the jaw clamps at the wood and the wood bites back; the shoulder seizes at the neck's command; I try to excavate the sound I hear in my mind from the wood. The clarity of the motif suffocates within the clutter of poor technique, but it is visible. It just needs some polishing.

Violent polishing.

The intuition remains sharp even as the mechanics atrophy but the boundary between intent and execution has frayed. No matter, it can be mended again. However, there is a razor-thin line where relief originates, and I find I can barely tolerate the crossing of it for long periods and end my session for that period.

As I matured in the discipline, I came to recognize the hard won skill in simplicity. And indeed it is hard won. Mastery is the ability to distill a thousand small movements into a single, simple, and potent voice.

For it is once, and only once, you can relinquish your thoughts to your medium that others can equivalently see that very vision. It is not exact, and never will be, but it is the essence. Therein lies the responsibility of any artist and their medium. The duty and distinction of any artist is to convey such a thing across multiple means of thought whether exacting or nebulous.

Research is not so unlike an instrument in this way.

You enter a field the same way you pick up the violin. You read widely enough to develop a sense for what a good question looks like, what a rigorous argument feels like, what an elegant methodology does; long before you can produce any of those things yourself. The literature does not meet you halfway. And like the bow, there are ways you are holding it wrong that you cannot yet detect, because the error lives beneath what you can currently read.

Take, for example, ferroelectric models; there is a landscape of models, each carrying their own lineage of reasoning, their own assumptions, their own orthodoxy and parameters. Where Preisach is phenomenological, a manifestation of the metrics of a hysteresis loop, concerning itself naturally with the graded variation of slopes, Landau-Khalatnikov is more physical, rooted in thermodynamics, oriented toward a single domain yielding an abrupt and idealistic coercive field. TDGL operates at a different level of abstraction entirely, concerned with the temporal and spatial evolution of an order parameter. Each one is a different way of holding the bow.

A researcher can inherit any one of these models, implement it, produce results, publish and never fully reckon with why that model and not another. The lineage, however, resides elsewhere: in the papers that derived it, in the researchers who understood the assumptions deeply enough to know where the cracks begin to form. To come to it through acceleration, through access rather than transmission, is to risk using Preisach where TDGL was warranted, or vice versa. The error lives beneath what you can currently read.

The bow is being held...awkwardly. The sound seems fine. Until it doesn't.

But something is shifting. The conditions around that gap are changing in ways that have no real precedent. Access to knowledge has never been broader, the tools never the more powerful, the distance between a naive question and a sophisticated one never shorter to travel. What happens then; not to the knowledge, but to the knower?

I think, and I say this with some uncertainty, as the conditions change, as tools compress the distance between the senses and the hand, the knower acts without fully understanding why. They can produce without being able to account for the production. The orthodoxy gets skipped, the correction never comes, and what's lost isn't necessarily the output but rather the lineage of reasoning underneath it. There is some degree of ignorance abstracted into every detail, every technique, every conclusion, that is, until it is arrived at. Or until they run into the problem again.

Chiefly, this asymmetry is a condition of anyone serious about a craft. For anyone pursuing any state of the art, gazing at a blank medium, there is a moment where the medium gazes back...something almost civilizational in scale for such an isolated contribution.

Is it then curiosity, or stubbornness, or the simple, haunting realization that the medium, whether wood or model, will eventually demand an account of the hands that held it?

Perhaps all three.
